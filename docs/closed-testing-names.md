# Closed testing: era names for approval

Names only. No game code, no icons, version stays 0.0.22.

Row titles stay technology names, the way Microprocessors and Graphics Cards
already do. Company and product names inside the history are parody. Model
numbers and the specs below are the real ones. Real people stay real when a
card credits an inventor. Managers stay fictional job titles.

Makers already in the Museum, reused here: Inland Business Machines (the
tabulator company), Chipwright, Novidia, Orchard, Calcicom.

## Calls made on purpose

- Ledger I is the Census machine, which is the mainframe the later generation
  list starts from. The Ferranti Mark 1 was delivered in Manchester in
  February 1951, earlier than the UNIVAC dedication. It can be the second
  Museum card if the row should open on the first commercial delivery
  instead.
- The console row opens on the 1977 Paddle Arts 2600, after Home Computers,
  because the Orchard II shipped on 10 June 1977 and the 2600 on 11 September
  1977. The 1983 and 1994 consoles stay on that same row.
- The Orchard Handset reuses Orchard, already the Apple parody. The name
  iPhone is not used.
- Tensor Foundry is not named Google. The TPU facts stay real.
- Dial-up Web has no parody of CERN. The 1994 half is the browser and the modem.

## Microprocessor generations

Same row as today (`cpu`, c. 1971). One model per generation, on the years
already in the design notes.

| Generation | Year | Parody | Maps to | Specs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4-bit calculator chip | 1971 | Chipwright 4004 | Intel 4004, for Calcicom | About 2,300 transistors, 740 kHz, 4-bit. Already on the Chipwright 4004 Museum card |
| 8-bit kit chip | 1974 | Chipwright 8080 | Intel 8080, April 1974 | 8-bit, 2 MHz, NMOS, 40-pin. The chip in the first hobby kits |
| 16-bit | 1978 | Chipwright 8086 | Intel 8086, 8 June 1978 | 16-bit, 5 to 10 MHz, about 29,000 transistors |
| 32-bit | 1985 | Chipwright 386 | Intel 80386, 17 October 1985 | 32-bit, first sold at 16 MHz, about 275,000 transistors |
| 64-bit | 2003 | Alder 64 | AMD Opteron, April 2003 | First chips to run the 64-bit PC instruction set. 130 nm. The desktop chip followed in September 2003 |
| Dual-core | 2005 | Chipwright Dual | Intel Pentium D, May 2005 | Two cores in one package. First desktop parts at 2.8 GHz. Alder shipped a two-core chip the same month |
| Many-core chiplets | 2017 | Alder 32 | AMD EPYC, 20 June 2017 | Up to 32 cores, four chips on one package, DDR4. The 2019 follow-on split compute chips from a separate I/O chip |

Alder Micro Devices is the new maker for the chips Chipwright did not ship
first.
